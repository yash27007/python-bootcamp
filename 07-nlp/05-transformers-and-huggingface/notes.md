# 05 – Transformers & HuggingFace

## Problem

[`06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`](../../06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers/notes.md) built a Transformer encoder from scratch — self-attention derived in NumPy, then a full encoder block trained in Keras — and reached 87.48% test accuracy on IMDB sentiment classification with random-initialized weights, trained on that one dataset alone. [`07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`](../04-deep-learning-nlp/notes.md) did the same thing architecturally simpler (`Embedding` + `LSTM`, no attention), also from random initialization, on 20,000 IMDB reviews, reaching 86.94%. Both notebooks prove the *mechanism* works — attention and recurrence are both trainable, differentiable operations that can learn to classify text. But both also share one assumption baked into "train from random weights": **every ounce of linguistic knowledge the model ends up with had to be learned from that one task's labeled training set, with nothing carried over from anywhere else.**

Real language — grammar, word meaning, idiom, common-sense associations between concepts — does not need to be re-derived per task. A model pretrained on a very large, general text corpus (with no task labels — via a self-supervised objective like "predict the masked word") already encodes a great deal of that knowledge before it ever sees a single labeled sentiment-classification example. The problem this topic solves: **how do you reuse that pretrained knowledge for a new task, instead of training a Transformer's attention weights from scratch every time, on however much labeled data that one task happens to have?**

## Intuition

Training `06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`'s classifier from scratch is like teaching someone a foreign language and how to judge movie reviews *at the same time*, using only 25,000 movie reviews as the entire source of language exposure. It works — people (and models) can learn a lot from 25,000 examples — but it's an inefficient way to teach language: most of what makes "this movie was surprisingly delightful" different from "this movie was surprisingly bad" is ordinary vocabulary and grammar knowledge that has nothing to do with movies specifically.

**Pretraining** is the alternative: first, train a large Transformer on a self-supervised objective (e.g. "guess the word hidden by a `[MASK]` token") over a huge, general, unlabeled text corpus — billions of words, no task labels needed, because the "labels" are just other words already in the text. This teaches the model grammar, common word associations, and a great deal of general "what typically follows what" knowledge. **Fine-tuning** then takes that pretrained model and continues training it — usually for only a few epochs, on comparatively little labeled data — on the *specific* task actually wanted (sentiment classification, here). The pretrained weights are the starting point, not random noise; fine-tuning only has to *adapt* existing knowledge to a new task, not build language understanding from nothing.

## Why simpler approaches fail

Training a Transformer (or LSTM) from randomly-initialized weights, on one task's labeled dataset alone, is exactly what both of this repository's existing deep-learning-NLP topics did — and their own results are the direct evidence for what gets left on the table:

- **`07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`**: `Embedding(10000, 32) → LSTM(32) → Dense(1, sigmoid)`, trained from scratch on 20,000 IMDB reviews for 8 epochs, reached **86.94% test accuracy** — but visibly overfit in the process (validation accuracy peaked at epoch 2 then declined as validation loss rose from 0.30 to 0.46), because a 320,000-parameter embedding matrix alone has to learn *both* general word representations *and* sentiment-specific structure from only 20,000 labeled examples.
- **`06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`**: a single Transformer encoder block, trained from scratch on the full IMDB training set, reached **87.48% test accuracy** — better, but still trained every one of its self-attention projection matrices ($W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$ per head) from random initialization, using only IMDB's ~25,000 reviews as the sole source of whatever grammar and word-association knowledge the model ends up with.

Both numbers are respectable, but both had to spend a large fraction of their limited labeled data budget on re-deriving basic language structure that a general-purpose pretrained model already has. This is the direct, "why simpler fails" cost of training from scratch: **every task starts from zero, no matter how many other tasks or how much other general text exists in the world** — training from scratch throws away all of that, every single time, and pays for it in more labeled data and more overfitting risk on datasets as small as this topic uses (see "Experiment," below, where fine-tuning on **50x less labeled data** than `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp` used is directly measured).

## Conceptual foundation

*(This topic substitutes a Conceptual-foundation section for a from-derivation Mathematical-foundation section, per `AGENTS.md`'s documented substitution allowance — the underlying attention mathematics is not new here and is cited, not re-derived, in "From-scratch implementation" below. What follows is new conceptual material specific to this topic: tokenization strategy and the pretrain/fine-tune paradigm.)*

### Subword tokenization: the OOV-vs-sequence-length tradeoff

Every model needs a fixed, finite vocabulary of tokens it can look up in its embedding table. There are three ways to define what a "token" is, and each makes a different tradeoff:

- **Word-level tokenization** (what `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s Keras `Embedding` uses: `VOCAB_SIZE=10000` most-frequent words): every distinct word is one token. This keeps sequences short (one token per word), but the vocabulary must be capped at some finite size, and **any word not in that vocabulary becomes an unknown-word placeholder (`<UNK>`/`<OOV>`)** — the model loses all information about that word's identity. Rare words, typos, novel compounds, and morphological variants (`"unbelievably"`, `"tokenization"`) are exactly the words most likely to fall outside a size-capped vocabulary, so this failure mode is common, not rare.
- **Character-level tokenization**: every individual character is a token. This has *no* out-of-vocabulary problem at all — any string is representable as a sequence of characters from a tiny, fixed alphabet — but it makes sequences dramatically longer (a 10-word sentence might be 50+ characters), and because self-attention's cost is $O(n^2)$ in sequence length (`06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`, "Failure modes"), quintupling sequence length roughly *25x*'s the attention computation for the same sentence.
- **Subword tokenization (BPE / WordPiece)**: a middle ground, learned from a large corpus rather than hand-designed. Starting from individual characters, the algorithm repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols into a new single symbol (Byte-Pair Encoding, BPE) or the pair whose merge most increases the training corpus's likelihood under a simple language model (WordPiece, used by BERT/DistilBERT — the notebook uses this variant), until a target vocabulary size is reached. The result: common whole words (`"the"`, `"movie"`, `"transformers"`) end up as single tokens, exactly like word-level tokenization for the common case, while rare or compound words decompose into a handful of known sub-pieces (`"unbelievably"` → `un`, `##bel`, `##ie`, `##va`, `##bly`) instead of becoming one opaque `<UNK>`.

This directly resolves the tradeoff: subword tokenization has **no out-of-vocabulary problem** (any string decomposes into some sequence of known sub-pieces, in the worst case down to individual characters/bytes, which are always in the vocabulary) while keeping **sequences close to word-level length** for ordinary text, since most words are common enough to stay whole. The price is that rare-word sequences are a little longer than pure word-level tokenization (a few sub-tokens instead of one `<UNK>`) — a small, bounded cost, paid only on the words that would otherwise have been unrecoverable anyway.

### Pretrain-then-fine-tune as transfer learning

This is the NLP instance of a more general idea, **transfer learning**: train a model once on a large, general source task (here: self-supervised masked-language-modeling on a huge unlabeled text corpus), then reuse most of its learned weights as the starting point for a different, usually smaller, target task (here: binary sentiment classification), rather than starting that target task's weights from random initialization.

Concretely, for an encoder-only Transformer like DistilBERT:
1. **Pretraining** (done once, by HuggingFace/the model's original authors, not by this notebook): the full Transformer — the same self-attention/feed-forward/layer-norm blocks from `06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`'s "Mathematical foundation" — is trained on a masked-language-modeling objective over a large text corpus, learning general $W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$ projections and token embeddings.
2. **Fine-tuning** (what this topic's notebook does): the pretrained Transformer's weights are loaded as the *starting point*, a new, randomly-initialized classification head (one small `Dense`-equivalent layer mapping the pooled representation to 2 sentiment classes) is attached on top, and the whole model — pretrained weights included, not frozen — continues training via ordinary backpropagation on the small labeled task dataset, for a few epochs, at a small learning rate.

Because only the classification head starts random — every self-attention weight starts from a state that already encodes general language structure — fine-tuning typically needs far less labeled data and far fewer epochs than training the same architecture from scratch would, to reach comparable or better accuracy. The "Experiment" section below measures this directly: fine-tuning on 400 labeled reviews (2% of `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s 20,000) against that topic's from-scratch result.

### What `pipeline()` does under the hood

HuggingFace's `pipeline("sentiment-analysis")` (used in "Practical implementation," below) is not a new mechanism — it is exactly three steps wrapped into one call:

1. **Tokenize**: run the model's associated tokenizer (subword tokenization, above) over the raw input text, producing `input_ids` (integer token indices) and an `attention_mask` (marking real tokens vs. padding).
2. **Forward pass**: feed `input_ids`/`attention_mask` through the pretrained Transformer's forward pass — the exact self-attention/feed-forward stack from `06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers` — producing raw output logits for each class.
3. **Decode**: apply `softmax` to turn logits into probabilities, `argmax` to pick the highest-probability class, and look up that class index in the model's `id2label` mapping to produce a human-readable label (`"POSITIVE"`/`"NEGATIVE"`) and confidence score.

The notebook runs these three steps manually, side by side with the one-line `pipeline()` call, and confirms both produce identical output — `pipeline()` is convenience packaging around the same three steps, not a different computation.

## Algorithm

1. Load a **tokenizer** matched to the pretrained model being used (same vocabulary, same subword-merge rules the model was pretrained with — see "Failure modes" for what happens if these are mismatched).
2. Tokenize input text into `input_ids` + `attention_mask`, padded/truncated to a fixed length (the same shape-constraint idea as `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s `pad_sequences`, "Mathematical foundation").
3. For **inference-only use** (Part 2 of the notebook): feed `input_ids`/`attention_mask` through the pretrained model's forward pass, decode logits to a label — this is what `pipeline()` automates.
4. For **fine-tuning** (Part 3 of the notebook): attach a new, randomly-initialized task-specific head to the pretrained model's output; train the whole model (pretrained weights + new head) with ordinary gradient descent on the small labeled task dataset, for a few epochs, at a small learning rate (`3e-5` here — noticeably smaller than the `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp` model's default Adam rate, since pretrained weights should be nudged, not overwritten — see "Failure modes," catastrophic forgetting).
5. Evaluate on held-out labeled data; compare against a from-scratch-trained baseline on a comparable task (this topic's "Experiment").

## From-scratch implementation

This topic deliberately does **not** re-derive attention mechanics. [`06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`](../../06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers/notes.md) already implements scaled dot-product self-attention from scratch in NumPy (Q/K/V projections, `softmax(QK^T/√d_k)V`, verified against a toy 6-token sequence and visualized as a heatmap) and builds a full Transformer encoder block (multi-head attention, residual connections, layer normalization, position-wise feed-forward) in Keras. Every pretrained model used in this topic's "Practical implementation" — `distilbert-base-uncased`, `distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english` — is built from *exactly that same mechanism*, stacked and scaled up (6 encoder layers, 12 attention heads, 768-dimensional embeddings for DistilBERT), with only two genuinely new ingredients this topic adds on top:

1. **Subword tokenization** feeding the model's input (see "Conceptual foundation" above) — `06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`'s notebook used pre-integer-encoded IMDB data (Keras's built-in word-index encoding), not a learned subword vocabulary.
2. **Pretrained weights** filling every $W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$ and feed-forward matrix, learned from a large-scale pretraining run this repository did not perform, instead of the from-scratch topic's fixed-seed-random (Part 1) or freshly-initialized-then-IMDB-trained (Part 2) weights.

Re-implementing multi-head self-attention in NumPy a second time here, with pretrained numbers substituted in, would repeat `06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`'s mechanics almost verbatim without adding insight — exactly the same reasoning `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s "From-scratch implementation" used for its own composition of already-derived mechanics. The from-scratch value in *this* topic is entirely in Part 1/2 of the notebook and "Conceptual foundation" above: seeing real WordPiece subword splits, and understanding what loading pretrained weights actually replaces (random initialization) versus what it leaves unchanged (the attention/feed-forward computation itself).

## Practical implementation

`transformers-and-huggingface.ipynb`, all three parts real HuggingFace code, actually executed, on small models only (`distilbert-base-uncased`, 66M parameters):

- **Part 1 — Tokenizer**: `AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilbert-base-uncased")` applied to a real sentence, printing the actual subword split (`tokenizer.tokenize(...)`), token count vs. naive whitespace word count, `input_ids`, and a round-trip decode — directly demonstrating "Conceptual foundation"'s WordPiece behavior on real (not toy/synthetic) input.
- **Part 2 — `pipeline()`**: `pipeline("sentiment-analysis", model="distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english")` run on three held-out sentences, compared side-by-side against the manual tokenize → forward-pass → softmax/argmax/`id2label` steps run explicitly with `AutoTokenizer` + `AutoModelForSequenceClassification` — confirming both produce identical labels/scores, making "what `pipeline()` does under the hood" concrete rather than descriptive.
- **Part 3 — Fine-tuning**: `AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("distilbert-base-uncased", num_labels=2)` (pretrained encoder + a fresh random classification head) fine-tuned with a plain PyTorch training loop (`AdamW`, explicit `.backward()`/`.step()` calls, no `Trainer` abstraction — kept visible on purpose, the same "see the actual gradient steps" spirit as `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s Keras `.fit()` call, made even more explicit) on a 400-review IMDB subset, evaluated every epoch on a held-out 200-review subset, and compared directly against `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s from-scratch 86.94% baseline — reaching **78.00%** eval accuracy after 3 epochs (up from 46.00% before fine-tuning), in 324s of CPU training (full trajectory and interpretation in "Experiment," below).

## Experiment

**Hypothesis (stated before running):** fine-tuning pretrained DistilBERT should reach competitive sentiment-classification accuracy despite using far less labeled data (400 reviews) than `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s from-scratch baseline (20,000 reviews), because it starts from real linguistic knowledge rather than random weights — the "Why simpler approaches fail" argument, made testable.

**Setup:** `distilbert-base-uncased` + a random classification head, fine-tuned on 400 IMDB training reviews (2% of the from-scratch baseline's training set), 96-token max length, batch size 16, learning rate 3e-5, 3 epochs, evaluated after every epoch on a held-out 200-review IMDB test subset (see `transformers-and-huggingface.ipynb`, Part 3, for the exact code).

**Result:** eval accuracy climbed every epoch — **46.00%** before any fine-tuning (pretrained encoder + untrained random classification head, chance-level as expected), **55.00%** after epoch 1, **70.50%** after epoch 2, **78.00%** after epoch 3 — for a total fine-tuning wall time of 324s (~5.4 minutes) on CPU. Training loss fell monotonically alongside it (0.6951 → 0.6524 → 0.4309), with no sign of the accuracy-declining-while-loss-falls overfitting signature within these 3 epochs. Compared against `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s 86.94% test accuracy from 20,000 training reviews and 8 epochs.

**Interpretation:** this result supports the hypothesis. 78.00% accuracy from 400 labeled reviews (2% of the from-scratch baseline's 20,000) and 3 epochs is a substantial result for that little labeled data and training time — reaching within ~9 points of the from-scratch model's 86.94% despite a 50x smaller training set. The accuracy trend was still rising, not plateaued or declining, at epoch 3 (unlike `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s from-scratch model, which had already started overfitting — validation accuracy declining — by its epoch 2 out of 8), suggesting a few more epochs or a somewhat larger fine-tuning subset would likely close more of the remaining gap to 86.94%, an option the from-scratch model did not have (it was already past its peak).

**Limitations:** one fine-tuning configuration (no hyperparameter search), a 400/200-review train/eval split rather than the full IMDB dataset (kept small deliberately, per `AGENTS.md`'s "no heavy/long-running training" constraint and this phase's few-minutes CPU runtime budget), the eval subset doubles as both per-epoch monitoring set and the final reported metric rather than using separate validation/test splits, and only 3 epochs were run while accuracy was still rising — so the comparison against `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s 86.94% should be read as an order-of-magnitude, label-efficiency comparison, not a controlled, matched-protocol benchmark.

## Failure modes

- **Catastrophic forgetting.** Fine-tuning updates *every* weight in the pretrained model, not just the new classification head. Too high a learning rate, or too many epochs on a narrow task, can overwrite the general language knowledge pretraining produced — the model becomes very good at the fine-tuning task specifically while losing generalization ability it had before. This is why fine-tuning learning rates (`3e-5` here) are typically much smaller than from-scratch training rates, and why fine-tuning runs for few epochs rather than many.
- **Tokenizer/model mismatch.** A pretrained model's weights were learned against one specific vocabulary and subword-merge ruleset. Tokenizing input with a *different* tokenizer (even a superficially similar one, e.g. a different model's WordPiece vocabulary) produces `input_ids` that index into the wrong embedding rows — the model receives numbers that don't mean what it was trained to expect, silently, with no error raised. `AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(same_model_name)` guarantees the match; hand-rolling or reusing a tokenizer from a different checkpoint does not.
- **Overfitting fast on small fine-tuning data.** This topic's own "Experiment" did *not* show the decline within its 3 epochs (eval accuracy was still rising at epoch 3: 46.00% → 55.00% → 70.50% → 78.00%) — but that is a matter of stopping early, not immunity to the failure mode: `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s from-scratch model, trained on 50x more labeled data, had already started declining by its epoch 2 out of 8. A 66M-parameter pretrained model fine-tuned on only 400 examples for enough additional epochs would be expected to hit the same "training loss keeps falling, eval accuracy peaks then degrades" pattern eventually — the risk scales with how many more epochs are run on how little data, which is exactly why fine-tuning recipes favor few epochs and close validation monitoring rather than "train until loss stops decreasing."
- **Quadratic attention cost persists.** Every failure mode from `06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers` ("Failure modes") still applies — pretraining and fine-tuning change *what the weights are*, not the $O(n^2)$ attention cost in sequence length, still capped at `MAX_LEN=96` tokens in this topic's fine-tuning run.

## Real-world usage

Pretrain-then-fine-tune is the default architecture pattern in production NLP, not an alternative to consider: sentiment analysis, intent classification, named-entity recognition, and document classification systems overwhelmingly start from a pretrained checkpoint (BERT/DistilBERT/RoBERTa-family for encoder tasks, GPT-family for generation) and fine-tune, rather than training a Transformer from random weights per task — the labeled-data and compute savings measured in this topic's "Experiment" (competitive accuracy from a small fraction of the labeled data `07-nlp/04-deep-learning-nlp`'s from-scratch model needed) are exactly why. HuggingFace's `transformers` library (`AutoTokenizer`, `AutoModelForSequenceClassification`, `pipeline()`) is the de facto standard tooling for this pattern, with a public model hub hosting the pretrained checkpoints so most teams never run the (expensive, days-to-weeks-of-compute) pretraining step themselves — only fine-tuning, which is what this topic's notebook runs in minutes on CPU.

## Mental model

**A from-scratch Transformer starts every task from zero and has to spend its entire labeled-data budget learning both "how language works" and "how to do this specific task." A pretrained-then-fine-tuned Transformer starts already knowing "how language works" — from a much larger, separate, unlabeled training run — and spends its (much smaller) labeled-data budget only on "how to do this specific task."** The attention mechanism underneath is unchanged either way (`06-deep-learning/05-attention-transformers`); subword tokenization and pretrained weights are the two things that change, and both exist specifically to make reuse — of vocabulary coverage, and of learned knowledge — possible instead of mandatory-to-relearn per task.
